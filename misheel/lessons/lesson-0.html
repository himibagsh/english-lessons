The lost hour

Freya Lund on what happened when her school started an hour later

A

I have never been a morning person, but until last year I assumed that was a personality trait rather than a problem. Then my school moved the start of the day from eight o'clock to nine, as part of a trial, and something I had not expected happened: my marks went up. Not dramatically, and not in every subject — but enough that I began reading about why.

B

Scientists who study sleep talk about the body clock, the internal timer that tells us when to feel tired. In most people it settles into a stable pattern by adulthood. In teenagers, though, it shifts: the signal to sleep arrives one to two hours later than it did in childhood, and later than it will in adulthood. The effect is well documented, and it is biological rather than a matter of willpower. A teenager told to sleep at ten and wake at six is not being lazy when they fail; they are being asked to sleep at a time their body has not yet reached.

C

What surprised me most was how much of the research is about light rather than about tiredness. Screens are usually blamed, and the blue light they emit19 does delay the body clock — but the studies I read suggested that the content matters more than the light. A message that makes you anxious will keep you awake far longer than the brightness of the screen that delivered it. Daylight in the morning turned out to matter more than darkness at night: students who walked to school slept better than those who were driven, whatever time they had gone to bed.

D

Schools that have moved their start times report fewer late arrivals, fewer absences and, in several cases, better results — though not every trial has found the same thing, and the schools that volunteer for these trials may not be typical. My own school has now made the nine o'clock start permanent. I would like to claim that I use the extra hour wisely. In fact I mostly sleep through it, which, according to everything I have read, is exactly the point.

What goes home on Monday

8 minutes

Six short pieces of paper, one for each day. Fifteen minutes a day, after school, and the whole thing comes back next Monday. Nothing on it is new — every part uses the text you have just read.

MON — ten words, written out with a phrase and a sentence of your own
TUE — read the article again, then four multiple-choice questions
WED — Murphy Unit 69, countable and uncountable
THU — Murphy Unit 70, then the articles sheet
FRI — the email, 120 to 160 words, fifteen minutes on a clock
SAT — read the article out loud twice, and check your own email against the list

The one rule: do the Friday email to the clock and then stop. Fifteen minutes, not forty. Finishing on time is the skill being practised, not finishing well.
