The note above the note

Anna Petrov spends ten weeks learning to throat sing

A

Before I moved to Mongolia last year, I had heard recordings of khöömii, the style of singing in which one person produces two notes at the same time. I assumed it was something you were either born with or you were not. So when a colleague mentioned that a teacher in my neighbourhood accepted adult beginners, I signed up — mainly to prove myself wrong. The advertisement promised that students would perform in front of an audience after ten lessons. What I got instead was something I had not expected: an education in how to listen.

B

My teacher, Batbayar, began by explaining where the sound comes from. Khöömii is usually translated as “throat singing”, although “overtone singing” would be more accurate: the singer holds one low note and slowly changes the shape of the mouth, so that a second, whistle-like note appears above it. Herders in the west of the country are generally credited with developing the technique, and it has always been associated with the sounds of the natural world — wind crossing grass, water running over stones. Batbayar told me that his own teacher had learned it from an uncle, and that until quite recently almost nobody studied it in a classroom.

C

There were five of us in the group, ranging in age from 19 to 63. In the first three sessions we did not sing at all. Instead we practised breathing, and listened to recordings until we could tell one regional style from another. Batbayar was endlessly patient, which surprised me, as I had imagined that a teacher of a traditional art would be strict. He asked us to hum one note for as long as we could, then to move our tongues slowly forwards and backwards while humming. It sounds simple, and it is not: for a fortnight I produced nothing but a flat buzz. The others appeared to be progressing faster than I was, and I began to wonder whether I should stop.

D

Then, in the sixth lesson, it happened. Somewhere above my own voice a thin, clear note appeared, and the whole room turned round. I would love to tell you that I could then repeat it whenever I wanted; in fact it took another three weeks before I could find that note reliably.28 I am still a long way from performing, and I have quietly decided not to take part in the concert. But I hear ordinary sound differently now. I catch the higher notes hiding inside noises I used to ignore, from car engines to the wind against my window. Batbayar says that this is the real lesson, and that the singing follows.

Exercise 2 · Which section?

6 marks · new

This is an exercise type you have not met yet. Which section, A–D, contains each piece of information? A section can be used more than once.

Where the marks go

Two sections will always look possible. One gives an example; the other gives the reason, the feeling or the comparison that the statement actually asks for. Underline the key word in the statement first — why, feel, compare — and match that, not the topic.

Exercise 3 · Note-making

6 marks · new

Imagine you are giving a talk to your class about khöömii. Make short notes under each heading, using words from the article.

Read the heading twice

Whole marks are lost here for one reason: true facts written under the wrong heading. If the heading says difficulties, a date earns nothing. Notes only — no full sentences — but include a verb where the note needs one.
